# Rivals Own Pieces; Nobody Combines Them

No product found does what Carlo's Compass plans to do, but almost every single piece of it already exists somewhere, and two products deserve real respect. The closest rival for the first dataset is a free phone app, **"St Carlo: Eucharistic Miracles"**, launched in February 2026 by a Pennsylvania shrine together with Carlo's mother, with more than 13,000 downloads; it has a map of the miracles but no timeline, no web version, no sources per miracle, and closed data. The closest rival for the Bible side is not one product but a crowd: dozens of small 2025-2026 sites and apps that all put the same free dataset (OpenBible.info) on a map, most with almost no users. Among general history maps, TimeMap.org proves that "one slider at the bottom, click for a summary" delights people, and it also shows what they punish: wrong borders, a slider that is confusing to grab, and trouble on phones. Three things in the vision are open ground: an openly licensed Eucharistic miracles dataset, a Catholic-canon Bible map, and one engine that different communities fill with their own content. The main things to reconsider are the wording "Church-approved" for the miracles, how honest the border display is, how Bible places get dates and handle rival locations, and how much to expect from outside contributors. One caution about this research itself: the researchers read pages, store listings, and press coverage but did not use most of these products hands-on, so "none found" means none found, not proof that none exists.

## The miracle map already exists as an app, but the open dataset does not

Carlo's own catalogue is still online, and it is not a web product. The official site is a list of links to PDF panels grouped by country, **108 place entries under 20 countries** plus panels on saints, Our Lady, and miraculous communions, with no interactive map, no search, no timeline, and no licence or terms of use anywhere on the pages read ([miracolieucaristici.org](https://www.miracolieucaristici.org/en/liste/list.html)). The association offers the panels as free downloads for hosting the exhibition, which is not the same as permission to republish the text or photos ([carloacutis.com](https://carloacutis.com/en/association/mostra-miracoli-eucaristici)). One developer who rebuilt the catalogue in 2026 treats the content as copyrighted and keeps the data locked away rather than publishing it ([write-up](https://tomportfolio.netlify.app/posts/2026-05-27-eucharistic-miracles/)). That confirms the decision already in the vision: write original summaries, link to Carlo's panels, never copy them.

The real competitor is the Malvern app. The St. Carlo Acutis Shrine at Malvern Retreat House launched it on 14 February 2026 in collaboration with Antonia Acutis ([Vatican News](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/church/news/2026-02/st-carlo-acutis-eucharistic-miracles-new-mobile-app.print.html)). It offers the miracle stories from Carlo's site, an interactive map by country, search by category such as "doubting priest," Bible verses per miracle, pilgrimage links, and has **more than 13,000 downloads in 132 countries** ([NC Register](https://www.ncregister.com/features/how-st-carlo-acutis-inspired-this-eucharistic-miracles-app)). It is free, rated 5.0 from 24 ratings, and still maintained ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/6758726547)). What it does not have, as far as the coverage shows: a web version, a timeline of the miracles (its only timeline is of Carlo's life), any stated Church-approval status or source list per miracle, or reusable data. Nobody on the research team installed it, so its exact map behaviour is unverified. The honest pitch is therefore not "the first map of Carlo's miracles." It is "the first one on the web, with a timeline, with sources on every card, and with data anyone can reuse."

The web has only one working counterpart, eucharistic-miracles.net, which offers a table, a map, cards, search by country or city, filters, and a link to the original PDF for each miracle ([write-up](https://tomportfolio.netlify.app/posts/2026-05-27-eucharistic-miracles/)). Its code is public with no licence, and the miracle data is not in the repository ([GitHub](https://github.com/thsmale/eucharistic-miracles-ui)). A third attempt, SkySurfer's map, is unfinished and says markers are still to be added ([sky-surfer.com](https://www.sky-surfer.com/eucharisticmiraclesbystcarlosacutis)). A search of GitHub found about a dozen Eucharistic miracle repositories, all with zero or one star and mostly without a licence ([GitHub search](https://api.github.com/search/repositories?q=eucharistic+miracles&sort=stars)). **No openly licensed dataset of Eucharistic miracles with coordinates and sources was found anywhere.** A small, reviewed, CC BY-SA set of 10-20 miracles would be a genuine first.

Two neighbouring Catholic maps teach the most about design. The Relic Project, unveiled in November 2025, is a pin map of relics browsable by saint, location, or class, and it names Carlo as a patron and his catalogue as its model ([NC Register](https://www.ncregister.com/news/the-relic-project-world-first-map-of-relics)). The National Geographic map of Marian apparitions, built on Michael O'Neill's MiracleHunter data, is the only Catholic map found that shows approval level right in the legend, separating tradition-only, unconfirmed, bishop-approved, and Vatican-recognised sightings ([Big Think](https://bigthink.com/strange-maps/world-map-marian-apparitions)). No Eucharistic miracle product does the same. This matters for one line in the current mockup: the Explore checkbox reads "Church-approved sites worldwide." The researchers found no authoritative list giving the recognition status of each catalogued miracle, and their reading is that recognition is usually local (a bishop, a feast, a long-standing devotion) and differs case by case. That is the researchers' inference, not a sourced fact, but it is enough to reconsider a blanket label. A per-miracle recognition field with levels modelled on MiracleHunter's, each with its own citation, is both safer and more distinctive.

## Bible maps are everywhere; cited, calm, Catholic ones are not

Almost every Bible map traces back to one free dataset. OpenBible.info's Bible Geocoding data covers every place in the Protestant Bible, is free to use with credit (CC BY 4.0), and scores each proposed location by counting scholarly sources as votes, drawing on over seventy modern sources and more than 400 cited works ([GitHub repo](https://github.com/openbibleinfo/Bible-Geocoding-Data)). It is the best certainty and citation data in the field. Its limits are just as clear: on the site it is an alphabetical list with Google Earth downloads rather than a filterable map ([OpenBible Geo](https://www.openbible.info/geo/)), the repository has had **no update since November 2021** ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/openbibleinfo/Bible-Geocoding-Data)), it leaves out Tobit, Judith, and Maccabees, and it carries no dates at all ([Yahweh's World README](https://github.com/SuyangLiuPaul/Yahwehs-World)). The companion dataset for people and events, Theographic, is actively maintained and share-alike licensed (CC BY-SA 4.0, the same licence chosen for Carlo's Compass content), but it covers the 66-book King James Bible and follows a single conservative chronology ([README](https://github.com/robertrouse/theographic-bible-metadata)).

The established products each hold one piece. Bible Gateway's atlas follows the passage you are reading and marks uncertain places with a "?", but it sits behind a paid membership and shows a modern satellite view ([Bible Gateway support](https://www.biblegateway.com/support/KA-01410)). Accordance's atlas has layers the user switches on and off and animated routes, but costs $69.90 and does not run on phones ([Accordance](https://www.accordancebible.com/product/accordance-bible-atlas-version-2-2/)). Logos and its Catholic edition, Verbum, tie an atlas to a large paid library, and Logos users complain on the company's own forum that the map does not show where a place is when it opens ([Logos forum](https://community.logos.com/discussion/243709/my-biggest-annoyance-with-the-atlas)). These details come from search summaries rather than hands-on use, so treat them as likely rather than certain. Phone apps are small: the best-known free one has 4.3 stars from only 108 ratings and reviews complaining of "constant ad & game pop ups" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1150969326)). A free, ad-free, no-login web map that opens on the place searched answers the most common documented complaints.

The newcomers are the ones to study. A burst of nearly identical projects appeared in 2025-2026, most with zero to two GitHub stars, which suggests that putting OpenBible data on a map is now a weekend project and no longer a selling point by itself. Three stand out. **History Globe** is the nearest overall match: a free 3D globe with 1,231 Bible places, a time slider that moves borders and journeys, candidate sites for disputed places, and 21 languages. Its author drafts articles with AI help, checks them against sources, and labels every article that no historian has read ([README](https://github.com/ArVaViT/history-globe)). That is the same "AI drafts, a person reviews, the page says so" stance as the vision, though Carlo's Compass is stricter, since unreviewed content never reaches the site. **biblical-atlas** is the nearest match in construction: one YAML file per entity, automatic checks on the data, a static MapLibre site with no server, every fact carrying its source, and uncertain places drawn as zones, "never as a sure point" ([README](https://github.com/GeiserX/biblical-atlas)). It is in Spanish and has 11 stars. **bible-map** by lineabuildsclaws is a deliberately calm 2D atlas that shows major places first, based on how often the Bible mentions them, and saves obscure ones for close zoom ([README](https://github.com/lineabuildsclaws/bible-map)).

Two facts from these projects should change how the team thinks about Bible places later. First, one project counted that **740 of 1,332 located places, 55 percent, have more than one rival scholarly identification** in the OpenBible data ([Yahweh's World README](https://github.com/SuyangLiuPaul/Yahwehs-World)). A single dot with a certainty word hides that; the more careful projects show candidate sites or zones. Second, dates are the hard part. Because OpenBible has none and scholars disagree (the Exodus is placed around 1446 BC or around 1270 BC), one project steps through verses in Bible order instead of years, and another labels each date by where it came from: stated in Scripture, calculated, estimated from outside evidence, or unknown ([bible-timeline README](https://github.com/jeroen-leverman/bible-timeline)). The Gospel slice planned for the demo avoids most of this, but the Old Testament will not.

No Catholic interactive Bible map with cited pins was found. The one Catholic-framed newcomer, the Kerygma app, covers all 73 books but its atlas is a side feature of about 100 places inside an AI chat app ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6762185288)). Non-English Catholic products were barely searched, so this gap is probable rather than proven.

## One slider wins hearts; wrong borders lose them

TimeMap.org is the proof that the timeline idea works. Built by MapTiler and Old Maps Online, it lets anyone pick a year from 4000 BC onward with a slider along the bottom and click through to Wikipedia summaries ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/geography/maps/explore-world-history-interactive-map)). Its Hacker News discussion reached **1,306 points and 180 comments**, with praise like "I could spend hours on it" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42397550)). The same thread is a free list of what to avoid. People reported that the year control looked like a slider but behaved like a text box, that it failed on small screens and some Firefox setups, that overlapping markers were hard to tell apart, and that cities appeared before they were founded. Above all they found border mistakes in the countries they knew best: Portugal under Spain, Lithuania's dates off, missing states. Border errors are the biggest reputation risk for any timeline map, and a hackathon audience will look at its own country first.

The vision already avoids several of these: pins are hidden until their date is reached, the handle must look draggable, and borders are thin, muted, and marked approximate. Three additions are worth making. The border data the team chose warns in its own README that it is a work in progress and should be checked against other sources ([historical-basemaps](https://github.com/aourednik/historical-basemaps)), so with only four to six border years in the demo, the map should say which year it is showing, for example "borders as of 1200." The scrubber should be tested on a real phone and in Firefox before the demo. And the two-zone timeline (a Gospel zone and a miracles zone with a break) is new: no product found uses a split or stretched slider, so it is a differentiator but also untested, and worth showing to a few people before committing.

Running Reality shows the other lesson. It requires a citation and team review for every fact in its main world, the closest match to "every claim cited," yet reviewers say its breadth makes it hard to know where to start ([World History Commons](https://worldhistorycommons.org/running-reality)). The vision's empty first screen with one Explore button is the right answer to that. For later, OpenHistoricalMap is the cleanest long-term border source: its data is public domain, it works directly with MapLibre, and it was very active through 2026 ([OHM reuse guide](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/OpenHistoricalMap/Reuse)). Keeping it as the "later" option, as the vision does, is sound.

## Open files and packs are proven separately, never together for faith

The editing model has a strong precedent and a known weakness. Open States keeps one YAML file per person, takes changes by pull request, checks every file automatically, and has passed **12,000 commits** ([GitHub](https://github.com/openstates/people)). Pleiades, the scholarly gazetteer of over 42,000 ancient places, uses the same shape with different tools: a contributor drafts, submits for review, and an editor publishes ([Pleiades help](https://pleiades.stoa.org/help/better-locations)). Every project that demands a source for each claim gates publication behind a reviewer, so the vision is in line with scholarly practice. The weakness is people, not vandalism. Pleiades once built up a backlog of over 1,000 items waiting for review ([Pleiades blog](https://pleiades.jazkarta.com/news/blog/review-parties-in-december-and-january)). Open data on GitHub does not attract contributors by being there: the Islamic-world gazetteer al-Thurayya has open files and a single open pull request ([GitHub](https://github.com/althurayya/althurayya.github.io)), and the Atlas of Quran invited contributions but was last updated in May 2020 ([Atlas of Quran](https://atlasofquran.github.io)). Non-technical people struggle with file syntax and with the idea of branches, and still need someone technical to press merge ([Thread engineering blog](https://medium.com/thread-engineering/teaching-non-engineers-how-to-contribute-code-2e85411ab464)). The fixes are consistent across sources: automatic checks, a template file with examples, an "edit this pin" link that opens the right file in GitHub's web editor, and a plain form for people who will never use GitHub. The vision's "friendlier editor later" should be read as "required before expecting outside edits," and for the hackathon the team's own entries are what will exist.

The engine-and-packs idea is also proven in structure. Peripleo, an open-source map template from the British Library, keeps the engine in one repository and the settings and data in another; changing the dataset means changing a filename in a config file ([GitHub](https://github.com/britishlibrary/peripleo)). The planned `engine/` plus `packs/bible-catholic/` layout follows the same pattern. Existing templates stop short in one place: changing the look means editing code, so colors named by role inside a pack would go further than they do. CollectionBuilder offers a tidy model for licences, with code under MIT, documentation under CC BY-SA, and each item carrying its own rights field ([CollectionBuilder](https://science.ecosyste.ms/projects/126077)); recording the source licence in each place file would keep Carlo's Compass just as clear.

What nobody has done is share one engine across faith communities. Every tradition has its own map, each built separately: al-Thurayya for the classical Islamic world, the Syriac Gazetteer for Syriac Christianity ([Syriaca](https://syriaca.org/geo/about.html)), BYU's scripture maps for Latter-day Saints, and others. The nearest thing to a multi-faith map is a single research team mapping Jewish, Christian, and Muslim pilgrimage in one dataset ([University of Fribourg](https://www.unifr.ch/art/fr/recherche/projets-de-recherche/holy-networks/database.html)). So goal 5 is open ground, but with a caveat: there is no evidence yet that other communities want it. It is a credible future, not a proven demand, and the pitch should say so.

## Conclusion

The research changes the story the team should tell. "A Bible map with a timeline" is now common, and "a map of Carlo's miracles" already has a well-connected app. What is rare is trust made visible: a short card where every sentence has a source, a location and a Church recognition that each state how sure they are, a reviewer's name and date, and data anyone can check and reuse. That combination was found nowhere, and it is the part of the vision that is hardest to copy in a weekend. It is also the part that costs the most effort per pin, which argues for a small, flawless set of miracles over a large one.

Three practical steps follow that are not yet in the vision. Email the Associazione Amici di Carlo Acutis (info@miracolieucaristici.org) to tell them about the project and ask their blessing, since the Malvern team went through Carlo's mother and no written reuse terms exist. Read the biblical-atlas repository before writing the data schema, because it has already solved one-file-per-place with automatic checks and uncertain places (its code is GPL-3.0, so learn from it rather than copy it). And treat maintenance as the real competitor: the common failure among these projects is not a rival winning but the author quietly stopping, which is exactly what a static site with plain files and no server is built to survive.
